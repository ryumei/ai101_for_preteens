Getting started

So what is AI, really?

You hear the word "AI" on the news and everywhere online.

But not many people can say what is actually happening inside one.

In this picture-story we are going to look inside the kind of AI you can chat with.

Once you know how it works, you can get along with it much better.

Getting started

Meet the LLM

You type a question, and it answers you in full sentences.

That kind of "text-writing AI" is called an LLM.

It stands for Large Language Model. A "model" here just means "a machine that copies how something works."

So the chatbot you have heard about? Underneath, it is an LLM.

How it works

The guess-the-next-word game

"Once upon a time, there lived..." — what word comes next?

Probably "an old man" or "a princess." Not "a fridge."

That guess-the-next-word game is the only thing an LLM does.

It picks a word, adds it on, then guesses the next one. Do that again and again, and a whole story appears.

3 / 18
How it works

It does not know what the words mean

The LLM does not know who "an old man" is. It is not answering because it understands.

It is choosing by how well words fit together — "after this word, that word usually comes."

That is not the same as a person talking because they understand.

But keep picking words that fit, and out comes writing that looks like understanding.

4 / 18
How it works

How did it get so good?

The LLM read an enormous amount of text from the internet.

Books, articles, things written by all kinds of people. Think of many libraries’ worth.

From all that, it learned patterns: "after this word, that kind of word tends to follow."

We call that "training."

5 / 18

Where did all that text come from?

This part is a bit harder. It is completely fine to skip it.

Mostly from text published on the internet: news, encyclopedias, someone’s blog, parts of books, program code. A great deal of it is ordinary writing by ordinary people.

Three things follow from that.

  1. It leans. The words and ideas that appear most often online are the ones it learns most. Rarer views it barely learns at all.
  2. It goes stale. Training costs time and money, so nobody rebuilds it daily. Some AIs only know text up to a certain date.
  3. Somebody wrote it. Every piece of that text had an author. Trace an AI answer back far enough and you reach a person’s words.

Today’s AIs are also tuned afterwards, by people telling them "this way of answering is good" and "this one is not." Even so, the lean in the original text does not vanish completely.

So when you ask an AI something, it is worth wondering: whose writing, about what, did this answer come out of?
How it works

Inside are billions of dials

Picture billions of tiny dials inside the LLM.

It reads some text and guesses the next word. When it guesses wrong, each dial gets nudged a little.

Repeat that billions of times, and you get a machine tuned to guess well. That is an LLM.

So "training" is not quite the same thing as you studying for a test.

6 / 18

So who turns all those dials?

This part is a bit harder. It is completely fine to skip it.

  1. Have a go. Show it a piece of text and let it guess the next word.
  2. Score it. Compare the guess with the real next word, and put a number on how far off it was.
  3. Trace the blame backwards. Which dials caused that error? Work backwards, from the output end towards the input end, to find out.
  4. Nudge them. Turn each dial a tiny bit, so that next time the guess lands closer.

1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → ... (billions of times!)

That trick of tracing the error backwards through the machine has a name: backpropagation.

No person sits there turning dials. A computer repeats this loop automatically: test, score, adjust, test again. That loop is what "training" actually is.

So even once it guesses well, the AI has not understood anything. It has just been tuned to score higher.
How it works

It is calculating, not thinking

When you read what an LLM writes, it really looks like it is thinking.

But what is happening inside is a calculation: which word is most likely to come next?

It does not feel happy or sad. It does not want anything.

Even when it says something kind, that is the result of a calculation that kind words fit there.

Good and bad at

Words are its strong suit

The LLM was trained on text — language turned into data.

So anything to do with words is where it is genuinely strong.

Writing something, saying it a different way, shortening a long text, translating into another language.

At those jobs it can sometimes be faster and smoother than a person.

8 / 18
Good and bad at

But it is not all-powerful

If someone speaks really well, you might assume they know everything.

Same with an LLM. It is so good with words that it looks capable of anything.

"Good with words" and "always right" are two different things.

It can get arithmetic wrong, or simply not know about recent events.

Good and bad at

It tells convincing lies

Remember: the LLM only strings together words that are likely to come next.

So when you ask about something it does not know, it strings together plausible-sounding words anyway.

And it says them with total confidence. Truth and made-up things come out wearing the same face.

This is the single most important thing to watch out for. Keep it in mind.

10 / 18

Why does it lie so confidently?

This part is a bit harder. It is completely fine to skip it.

An AI has no "I don’t know" button. Whenever it builds an answer, it is doing the one same job: pick the word most likely to come next.

So whether it is answering something it learned well or something it never saw, the words come out by exactly the same process. That is why truth and invention wear the same face.

Grown-ups have a name for these convincing inventions: hallucinations. As in, seeing something that is not there.

They show up most when you ask about:

  1. things barely written about online (a shop near you, your own school)
  2. numbers, dates and names, where being slightly off means being wrong
  3. things that do not exist at all (ask "what did you think of the book X?" about a book nobody wrote, and you will still get a review)

Researchers are working hard to reduce this, and more AIs now show you the sources they looked at. But it is not down to zero.

Which leaves one reliable way to tell: check it yourself.
Good and bad at

It memorised the mistakes too

Not everything the LLM read on the internet was correct.

Mistakes, things that are out of date, someone’s wrong assumption — it is all mixed in there.

And the LLM learned it mistakes and all.

So a smooth, instant answer is not the same as a correct one.

11 / 18
Good and bad at

It is echoing somebody’s opinion

Every piece of text the LLM learned from was written by a human being.

Which means what sits behind its answers is some person’s thoughts and opinions.

"The AI said so" does not mean "it is true." It is closer to "somebody wrote that somewhere."

People disagree with each other. Treat the AI’s answer as one more opinion in that mix.

Rule of thumb 1

AI gets things wrong. Check for yourself.

Treat an AI answer as a hint, never as the correct answer.

For anything that matters, look it up in a book, or ask a teacher or someone at home.

Get into the habit of pausing to ask "is that actually true?" and you become much harder to fool.

Checking feels like extra work, but it is the fastest route in the end.

13 / 18
Rule of thumb 2

Use it as a tool, well

What happens if you let an AI do your entire homework?

You get an answer. It might be wrong — and either way, none of it becomes your own ability.

An AI is a tool, like a pencil or a dictionary. You are the one using it.

Think it through yourself first, then ask for a hint when you are stuck. That is a good way to use it.

14 / 18
Rule of thumb 3

Keep yourself out of it

Whatever you type into an AI gets sent to a computer far away.

You cannot control who sees it or what it gets used for.

So do not type in real names, addresses, your school, or photos — yours or your friends’.

A good rule: only write things you would be fine showing to a stranger.

15 / 18
Rule of thumb 4

Talk to a person about the things that matter

An AI does not think. It has no feelings. It is running a calculation.

So when you are worried about something, or deciding something important, talk to a person, not an AI.

Family, teachers, friends. The ones who actually care about you are people.

AI is a handy tool. Now that you know how it works, use it well.

16 / 18
Rule of thumb 5

Rules are something we make together

An LLM leans toward "what lots of people wrote." A bit like a majority vote.

But smaller voices, and views that go against the crowd, matter too.

So how AI gets built and how it gets used should be decided by people talking it over together.

That idea has a name: governance. It is the same as your class agreeing on class rules.

17 / 18
One last thing

What is behind the convenience

This quiz, and every website you visit, runs on electricity.

AI especially: building the model and answering your questions both take a great deal of electricity.

"Chatting and electricity"? "Chatting and climate change"? It may not click straight away.

But convenient things are built on top of real resources. That is worth remembering.

So before you ask an AI everything — thinking for yourself matters too!

To sum up

  • An LLM just repeats "guess the next word." It does not know what the words mean
  • It learned patterns from an enormous amount of text — and learned the mistakes along with them
  • It is calculating, not thinking. There are no feelings in there
  • It is great with words, but not all-powerful, and it tells convincing lies
  • Its answers come from somebody’s writing, so how we use AI is something we decide together (governance)
